Use oxygen barrier PEX for any closed-loop heating system that contains iron or steel: a cast iron boiler, a steel expansion tank, cast iron circulators, steel panel radiators or black iron piping. Use non-barrier PEX for drinking water plumbing. Non-barrier PEX can be used for heating only when every wetted component in the loop is non-ferrous (bronze, brass, copper, stainless steel or plastic), or when the tubing is separated from the boiler by a heat exchanger. Getting this wrong does not cause a leak on day one. It causes slow corrosion that shows up years later as sludge, seized pumps and failed boilers.

What the barrier is for

PEX is slightly permeable to oxygen. In a closed heating loop the same water circulates for years, and it quickly loses its dissolved oxygen, which is what keeps iron and steel parts from rusting. If oxygen can pass through the tubing wall, the supply never runs out, and ferrous components corrode continuously. REHAU's technical bulletin on the subject describes exactly this: oxygen passes through an uncoated pipe wall, dissolves in the heating fluid and corrodes iron and steel pipes, valves, pumps and the boiler.

Barrier tubing adds a thin layer of EVOH (ethylene vinyl alcohol) that reduces oxygen permeation to a very low level. On most products the layer is on the outside of the tube or just under a protective outer coat. PEX-AL-PEX reaches the same result with its aluminum layer.

The standard to look for: DIN 4726

The benchmark used across the heating industry is the German standard DIN 4726. It limits oxygen permeation to no more than 0.32 mg per square meter of tube surface per day at 40°C (104°F). Boiler manufacturers commonly reference this limit in their installation and warranty conditions. In the US, ASTM F3253 covers PEX tubing with a polymeric oxygen barrier layer for hydronic systems.

To confirm what you have, read the print line on the tubing. Barrier tubing states the oxygen barrier or DIN 4726 compliance. Tubing for drinking water carries a potable water mark such as NSF-pw or NSF/ANSI 61.

Barrier and non-barrier tubing of the same type share the standard ASTM F876 temperature and pressure ratings (typically 100 psi at 180°F), so the barrier does not change what the tube can handle. It changes what the rest of the system can be made of.

When non-barrier PEX is acceptable for heating

  • Open systems. Where a water heater serves both the taps and a radiant loop with the same water, the tubing must be potable-rated, and all components (pump, valves, fittings) must be bronze, brass or stainless steel. This kind of system has its own code and health considerations and is not allowed everywhere.
  • All non-ferrous closed loops. A stainless steel or copper heat source, a stainless or bronze circulator, brass manifolds and a suitable expansion tank can run on non-barrier tubing. Check the heat source manufacturer's instructions first; many still call for barrier tubing.
  • Isolated loops. A plate heat exchanger separates the tubing loop from the boiler loop, so the boiler never sees the oxygenated water. The components on the tubing side must still be non-ferrous.

If you have inherited a system with non-barrier tubing already in a slab and a cast iron boiler, the usual fix is the third option: add a heat exchanger and non-ferrous components on the floor side.

Choosing a tubing size for radiant loops

Loop length is limited by the pressure drop the circulator has to overcome. Longer loops also mean a larger temperature difference between the start and end of the loop. Common design guidance for residential radiant floors is:

These are rules of thumb. A real design starts with the room-by-room heat loss, then sets tube spacing, water temperature and flow per loop. Keep loops on one manifold close to the same length so they balance easily. See oxygen barrier tubing and radiant heating manifolds.

PEX-A or PEX-B for heating?

Both are used. PEX-A is more flexible, which helps with tight tube spacing and cold-weather installation, and a kink can be repaired with a heat gun. PEX-B is stiffer. The connection method follows the tubing: expansion fittings for tubing listed to ASTM F1960, crimp or press fittings for the others, and compression adapters at most manifolds. Use the fittings the tubing manufacturer lists for its barrier product, because the barrier layer changes the outside surface and some fitting systems are approved only for particular tubing. Installation points that protect the barrier

  1. Keep tubing in its packaging and out of sunlight until it is installed. PEX has limited UV resistance, stated on the manufacturer's data sheet.
  2. Do not drag barrier tubing across rough concrete or rebar ends. The EVOH layer is thin and scratches reduce its effect.
  3. Respect the minimum bend radius printed in the manufacturer's instructions and use bend supports where the tube leaves a slab.
  4. Pressure test the loops before the pour or before closing the floor, and keep them under pressure during the pour so damage shows immediately.
  5. Sleeve the tubing where it crosses expansion joints and where it exits the slab.
  6. Fill and purge each loop separately, then treat the system water as the boiler manufacturer directs.

What else the system needs

A closed loop needs an expansion tank sized for the system volume, air elimination, a pressure relief valve at the heat source, and a circulator matched to the flow and head of the loops. Low-temperature radiant floors fed by a high-temperature boiler need a mixing device so the slab never receives full boiler temperature. When to call a professional

Laying tubing is physical work that a careful owner can do under a designer's layout. The connection to the heat source is different. Boiler piping, pressure relief, gas supply, venting and controls should be installed by a licensed heating contractor, and gas work requires a licensed professional in every jurisdiction. Have the heat loss and loop design done by someone qualified before buying tubing; correcting spacing or loop lengths after a slab is poured is not possible.
