F1960 expansion fittings are made for PEX-A tubing and should be used on PEX-B only when the tubing manufacturer states on the tube or in its instructions that it is approved for F1960 connections. F1807 brass insert fittings and F2159 plastic insert fittings work with PEX-A, PEX-B and PEX-C, and are secured with a copper crimp ring or a stainless steel clamp. The two families cannot be crossed: you do not crimp a ring onto an F1960 fitting, and you do not expand tubing over an F1807 or F2159 fitting. The print line on the tubing is the final authority on which fitting standards it is listed for.

What the standard numbers mean

The numbers are ASTM standards. Each one defines the dimensions and performance of one kind of fitting or ring, so parts from different manufacturers made to the same standard fit the same tubing.

Why F1960 depends on the tubing

An expansion joint relies on shape memory. The tubing and its ring are stretched, slipped over the fitting, and then shrink back until they grip it tightly. PEX-A, cross-linked by the peroxide (Engel) method, recovers quickly and fully. Standard PEX-B and PEX-C are stiffer and do not recover the same way; expanding tubing that is not designed for it can produce micro-cracks or a joint that never fully tightens.

The current edition of ASTM F1960 is written for PEX and PE-RT tubing in general, not for PEX-A by name, which is why approval comes from the tubing manufacturer's listing and not from the letter alone. Use the reinforcing ring the tubing manufacturer specifies, and in cold weather allow the longer shrink time given in the instructions before pressure testing.

F1807 versus F2159: brass or plastic insert

These two share the same joining method and the same tools. The difference is the fitting material.

Both are insert fittings, which means the fitting sits inside the tube and its bore is smaller than the tube's. F1960 fittings have a larger bore because the tube is expanded to go over them. On long runs with many fittings, or at 1/2 inch size, that difference in flow restriction can be worth accounting for in the pipe sizing.

Rings, clamps and tools

  • Copper crimp rings need a crimp tool sized for the ring (a 1/2 inch jaw for a 1/2 inch ring) and are checked with a go/no-go gauge. A joint that fails the gauge is cut out and remade.
  • Stainless clamps use a ratcheting clamp tool that releases only when the clamp is fully closed. One tool covers several sizes.
  • Expansion tools use a head sized for the tube. The ring is seated against the stop at the end of the tube, expanded the number of times the instructions give, and pushed fully onto the fitting shoulder.
  • Press sleeves. Some manufacturers, including Viega with PureFlow Press, supply insert fittings with a stainless sleeve attached that is pressed with a dedicated jaw. These follow their own standards and tools, and are not crimped.

Position matters on crimp and clamp joints: the ring sits 1/8 to 1/4 inch from the end of the tube so it compresses the tube over the barbs.

Common mixing questions

  1. Can I join PEX-A to PEX-B? Yes. Use an insert fitting (F1807 or F2159) with crimp rings or clamps on both sides. Do not use an expansion joint on the PEX-B side unless that tubing is marked F1960.
  2. Can I use crimp rings on PEX-A? Yes, if the tubing lists F1807 or F2159, which most does.
  3. Can I mix brands? Fittings made to the same ASTM standard are dimensionally compatible with tubing listed to it. Manufacturers' warranties often cover only their own complete system, so check the warranty terms if that matters to you.
  4. Can I crimp onto an F1960 fitting? No. Its barb profile and diameter are made for an expanded tube and ring.
  5. What about oxygen barrier tubing? The barrier layer changes the outside of the tube. Use only the fitting systems the barrier tubing manufacturer lists.
  6. PEX to copper? Use a transition fitting: sweat or press on the copper side and the matching PEX connection on the other. See copper to PEX adapters and our guide on connecting PEX to copper.

How to read the print line

Every length of PEX is printed at regular intervals with its size, material designation, pressure ratings and standards. Look for:

  • ASTM F876 (and often F877 or CSA B137.5): it is PEX tubing made to the standard dimensions and ratings.
  • The fitting standards: F1960, F1807, F2159, F2080. If a standard is not printed, the tubing is not listed for that fitting.
  • NSF-pw or NSF/ANSI 61: approved for drinking water.
  • A material code such as PEX 5306: the digits indicate chlorine resistance, UV resistance and hydrostatic design stress.

When to call a professional

PEX water lines are approachable for a capable owner, and many jurisdictions allow homeowners to do their own plumbing under a permit. Call a licensed plumber for a whole-house repipe, for connections at the water heater or water service, where the work will be concealed and must pass inspection, or if you are not able to pressure test the system before closing walls. Keep PEX the distance from water heater flues and recessed lights that the tubing manufacturer and code require, and never use plumbing PEX for gas.
