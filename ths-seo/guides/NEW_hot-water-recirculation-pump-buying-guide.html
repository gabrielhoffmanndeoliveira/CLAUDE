The right hot water recirculation pump depends on one thing first: whether your house has a dedicated return line running from the farthest fixture back to the water heater. If it does, you need a small bronze, stainless steel or composite circulator with a timer or temperature control. If it does not, you need a system that uses a crossover valve under the farthest sink and sends cooled water back through the cold line, or an on-demand pump that runs only when you ask for hot water. Everything else (brand, control type, connection size) follows from that answer.

What a recirculation system does

Without recirculation, the water sitting in the hot pipe between the heater and the faucet cools down. When you open the tap, that cooled water has to run down the drain before hot water arrives. A recirculation pump moves that cooled water back to the heater so the hot line stays warm. The pump is small: its job is to replace the heat the pipe loses, not to supply fixtures, so the flow involved is typically around a gallon per minute or less.

The trade-off is energy. A warm loop loses heat all the time the pump runs, and the water heater has to replace it. That is why the control matters as much as the pump.

Step 1: Find out which piping layout you have

  1. Look at the water heater. A dedicated return line is a third water pipe, usually 1/2 inch, that comes back to the heater and ties into the cold inlet or the drain port at the bottom of the tank. There is often an old pump or a check valve on it.
  2. Check the build. Return lines are common in larger and newer homes and are almost always present in homes that were sold with "instant hot water."
  3. No third pipe? Then you have a standard trunk-and-branch or manifold layout and need a crossover or on-demand system.

Pump material: potable water needs bronze, stainless or composite

A domestic hot water loop is an open system. Fresh, oxygen-rich water passes through it constantly, so a cast iron heating circulator will rust and discolor the water. A recirculation pump must have a bronze, stainless steel or engineered-composite body and must be rated for potable water. On pumps sold in the US, look for lead-free compliance (NSF/ANSI 372) and a potable water listing (NSF/ANSI 61) on the nameplate or data sheet.

Connection type is the other physical choice: sweat, threaded union, or flanged. Flanged pumps need a pair of matching pump flanges, sold separately. Match the connection size to the return line, which is normally 1/2 or 3/4 inch.

Controls: timer, temperature, learning or on-demand

  • Timer. The pump runs during set hours, such as mornings and evenings. Simple and inexpensive. Many small pumps are sold with a timer and line cord attached.
  • Aquastat or temperature mode. The pump stops when the return water is warm and restarts when it cools. Grundfos states that the ALPHA HWR in its temperature mode holds the return between about 95°F and 102°F.
  • Learning control. The control records when hot water is used and runs the pump ahead of those times. Taco's SmartPlug control, used with the SmartPlus-e, works this way, based on a week of observed use.
  • On-demand. A button or sensor starts the pump. It shuts off when the sensor at the fixture detects a temperature rise.

Energy codes also have a say. The residential provisions of the International Energy Conservation Code, which many states adopt with amendments, do not allow a circulation pump that runs continuously with no control, and they prohibit gravity (thermosiphon) loops. If the work is part of a permitted project, confirm the control requirement with your local building department.

Sizing: small is correct

A recirculation pump only has to overcome the friction of the loop at a low flow. For a typical house, the smallest pump in a manufacturer's recirculation range is usually enough, and a larger pump is not an upgrade. High velocity in hot copper tube causes erosion corrosion, which shows up as pinhole leaks at elbows and tees. The Copper Development Association's design guidance is to keep velocity at or below 5 feet per second for hot water up to 140°F, and lower above that. Grundfos limits its ALPHA COMFORT SYSTEM to a maximum of 1.2 gpm for this reason.

A larger pump is justified only for long loops, multiple branches with balancing valves, or commercial buildings, and those should be calculated from the loop's heat loss and head loss.

Recirculation with a tankless water heater

Tankless heaters add two conditions. First, the burner fires only above a minimum flow, so the pump has to move enough water to activate the heater, and the heater's manual will state the required pump performance. Second, many condensing tankless models have a recirculation mode of their own: some have a built-in pump and buffer tank, others have terminals for controlling an external pump. Use the manufacturer's recirculation diagram for the exact model. A pump on a timer that makes a tankless heater fire for hours every day shortens the life of the unit and can affect the warranty. What else goes into the installation

  • Check valve. A spring check valve on the return line (some pumps have one built in) stops cold water from being drawn backward through the return.
  • Isolation valves. Valves on both sides of the pump let you replace it without draining the system.
  • Pipe insulation. Insulating the hot supply and the return reduces the heat the loop loses and how often the pump has to run.
  • Air removal. Purge the loop after installation. Small circulators are water-lubricated and should not run dry.
  • Mixing valve. If the heater has a thermostatic mixing valve at its outlet, the return has to be piped to both the heater and the cold port of the mixing valve as shown in the valve's instructions. Piped wrong, the loop slowly goes cold.

Quick selection steps

  1. Confirm whether there is a dedicated return line.
  2. Check the water heater type. For tankless, read the recirculation section of the manual before choosing anything.
  3. Pick the system type from the table above.
  4. Choose a potable-rated pump body and the connection that matches your pipe.
  5. Choose the control: timer for predictable schedules, temperature or learning control for variable use, on-demand for the lowest energy use.
  6. Add the check valve, isolation valves and insulation.

When to call a professional

Installing a plug-in pump on an existing return line with unions or flanges is within reach of someone comfortable with soldering or press fittings. Call a licensed plumber if a return line has to be added, if the loop needs to be balanced across several branches, if the heater is a gas tankless unit that needs its recirculation mode set up, or if the pump needs a new electrical outlet. A new receptacle or hard-wired connection is work for a licensed electrician. Any change to water heater piping may require a permit.
