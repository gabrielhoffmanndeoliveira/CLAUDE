When a gas furnace won't heat, the problem is very often one of two small parts: the ignitor that lights the burners or the flame sensor that proves they're lit. Both are inexpensive and can be checked with basic tools. This guide covers how furnaces light, which part has failed, how to clean a flame sensor, how to test an ignitor, and how to choose a replacement, including Resideo (Honeywell) ignition modules.

How a modern gas furnace lights

Most modern furnaces light only when there's a call for heat, using one of three systems:

  • Hot surface ignition (HSI): the most common system in residential furnaces. A ceramic ignitor heats until it glows, then the control opens the gas valve and the burners light off the hot element.
  • Direct spark ignition (DSI): a high-voltage spark electrode lights the main burner directly. It's common on boilers, unit heaters and some furnaces.
  • Intermittent pilot (IP): a spark or hot surface lights a small pilot flame first. Once the pilot is proven, the main valve opens. This is common on older furnaces and many boilers.

Flame rectification: how the furnace knows it's lit

Every one of these systems needs proof of flame. A flame conducts a tiny electrical current, measured in millionths of an amp (microamps), and it conducts it better in one direction than the other. The control sends a voltage to the flame sensor rod, and the burner ground completes the circuit through the flame. If the control doesn't see that rectified current within a few seconds of opening the gas valve, it shuts the gas off.

Some systems use a separate flame rod (remote sense). Others use the same electrode to ignite and to sense (local sense or single-rod). This matters when you choose replacement parts.

Symptoms: is it the ignitor or the flame sensor?

Watch a heating cycle through the sight glass or with the burner door off. A typical HSI sequence is: inducer fan starts, pressure switch closes, ignitor glows, gas valve opens, burners light.

  • Burners light, then shut off after a few seconds: the classic dirty flame sensor symptom. The flame is there, but the control can't prove it. After several retries, many controls go into lockout.
  • No glow at all: the ignitor may be cracked or burned open. It could also mean the control isn't sending power to it, so test before you buy.
  • Ignitor glows, but burners never light: look at gas supply, the gas valve or the control, not the ignitor. Check that the gas is on at the appliance shut-off.
  • Spark but no light (DSI or pilot systems): check the electrode gap, the ignition cable and the ground connection.

Most furnace controls have a status LED that blinks a fault code. Compare the blink pattern with the chart on the inside of the furnace door before replacing anything.

Safety first

  • Turn off the power at the furnace service switch or the breaker. The ignitor runs on line voltage in most HSI furnaces, usually 120 VAC.
  • Turn off the gas at the manual shut-off valve on the furnace supply line.
  • Let the burner area cool for several minutes.
  • If you smell gas, don't touch any switches. Leave the building and call your gas utility from outside.
  • Take a photo of the wiring before you disconnect anything.
  • Follow your furnace manufacturer's instructions. If you're not comfortable with any step, call a licensed HVAC technician.

Where is the flame sensor on a furnace?

Remove the upper and lower furnace doors and find the burner assembly. The flame sensor is a thin, straight or bent metal rod set in a white porcelain insulator, mounted on a bracket so the tip sits in the burner flame. It's held in place by a single screw (often a 1/4-inch hex head) and has one wire attached. On many furnaces it sits at the far end of the burner row from the ignitor, so it only sees flame after the fire has carried across every burner.

How to clean a furnace flame sensor

  1. Turn off the power and gas, and let the burners cool.
  2. Disconnect the single wire from the sensor terminal.
  3. Remove the mounting screw and slide the sensor straight out. Handle it gently, because the porcelain can crack.
  4. Lightly rub the metal rod with a fine non-woven abrasive pad or very fine steel wool until the dull film is gone. You're removing oxide, not metal.
  5. Don't use coarse sandpaper or emery cloth. It leaves deep scratches that collect deposits faster, and it can leave grit on the rod.
  6. Wipe the rod with a clean, dry paper towel.
  7. Check the porcelain for cracks and the rod for heavy pitting or warping. If you see either, replace the sensor.
  8. Reinstall it in the same position, reconnect the wire, and make sure the burner assembly is properly grounded.
  9. Restore gas and power and run a full heating cycle. The burners should stay lit.

If the furnace still shuts down after a few seconds, check the ground wire, the burner flame (it should be mostly blue and touch the rod), and the sensor position. A sensor that fouls quickly can point to a combustion problem that needs a professional.

How to test a furnace ignitor

Visual check

With the power off, remove the ignitor (usually one or two screws and a plug). Never touch the element itself. Hold it by the ceramic base. Look for hairline cracks, white or chalky spots, and broken or burned leads. A cracked ignitor should be replaced even if it still glows.

Resistance (ohms) test

  1. Unplug the ignitor from the wiring harness.
  2. Set a multimeter to ohms and touch the probes to the two ignitor pins.
  3. An open circuit (OL or infinite) means the element is broken and must be replaced.
  4. A reading means the element is continuous. Compare it with the manufacturer's spec for that ignitor. Many 120 V silicon carbide ignitors read in the tens of ohms at room temperature, but ranges vary by model and material, so use the published figure rather than a general rule.

If the ignitor tests good but never glows, the control may not be sending voltage. Testing live 120 V circuits is a job for a qualified technician.

Furnace ignitor replacement: choosing the right part

Match the OEM part number first

The surest fit is the exact part listed in your furnace's parts list, or a direct replacement for it. Read the part number off the old ignitor or the furnace's rating plate. Silicon carbide vs silicon nitride

  • Silicon carbide: the traditional grey element. It works well but is brittle and sensitive to handling.
  • Silicon nitride: a newer, tougher element that's generally more resistant to breakage and heats up quickly.

Either can be the right choice, as long as the voltage, mounting and sensing type match your appliance. Never put a lower-voltage ignitor on a 120 V circuit, or the reverse.

Match the trial-for-ignition time, rod configuration and prepurge exactly. The trial time is a safety rating set by the appliance manufacturer, not a preference. Never substitute a module with a longer trial time than the original.

/U vs /B part numbers: some Resideo modules come in two versions. The /U suffix is individually boxed and the /B suffix is a bulk pack. The part and specifications are the same; only the packaging differs. For example, S8701A1114/B and S8801A1110/B are the bulk-pack versions of the modules above.

  • The job involves replacing a gas valve or ignition module, checking gas pressure or measuring flame current.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I clean my furnace flame sensor?

Many technicians check and clean it during annual furnace maintenance. If it fouls again within weeks, have the combustion and air supply checked.

Can I clean a flame sensor with sandpaper?

It's best not to. Coarse sandpaper scratches the rod and can leave grit behind. Use a fine non-woven abrasive pad or very fine steel wool, then wipe it clean.

How long does a furnace ignitor last?

It varies with type, cycling and handling. Silicon nitride ignitors are generally more durable than silicon carbide. Touching the element during service is a common cause of early failure.

Why does my furnace light and then shut off?

Most often it's a dirty or poorly grounded flame sensor. Other causes include a weak flame, a cracked sensor insulator, reversed polarity on the 120 V supply, or a failing control.
