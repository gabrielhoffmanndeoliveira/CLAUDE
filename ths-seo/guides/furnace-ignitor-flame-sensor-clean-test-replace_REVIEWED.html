You can clean a furnace flame sensor, but you should not clean a furnace ignitor. A hot surface ignitor is a brittle ceramic element: rubbing it with sandpaper, steel wool or even bare fingers can crack it or shorten its life, so an ignitor is inspected, tested with a multimeter and replaced if it fails. The part that does need cleaning is the flame sensor, the metal rod that proves the burners are lit. This guide shows how to tell the two apart, how to clean a flame sensor, how to test an ignitor and how to choose a replacement.

Ignitor or flame sensor: which one is the problem?

Watch one heating cycle through the sight glass in the burner door. A hot surface ignition furnace follows this order: inducer fan starts, pressure switch closes, ignitor glows, gas valve opens, burners light, flame sensor proves the flame, blower starts.

Burners light, then go out after a few secondsDirty or poorly grounded flame sensorClean the flame sensor

Most furnace controls also flash a fault code on a status LED. Read the code against the chart on the furnace door before replacing any part.

How does the furnace know the burners are lit?

A flame conducts a very small electrical current, a few microamps, and conducts it better in one direction. The control applies a voltage to the flame sensor rod, and the current returns through the flame to the grounded burner. This is called flame rectification. If the control does not see that current within a few seconds of opening the gas valve, it closes the valve. An oxide film on the rod, a cracked insulator or a poor burner ground reduces the current until the control can no longer prove the flame.

Some appliances use a separate flame rod (remote sense). Others ignite and sense through the same element (local sense). That difference matters when you choose a replacement ignitor.

Before you start

  • Switch off power at the furnace service switch or the breaker. On most hot surface ignition furnaces the ignitor runs on 120 V.
  • Close the manual gas shut-off on the furnace supply line.
  • Let the burner area cool.
  • Photograph the wiring before disconnecting anything.
  • If you smell gas, do not operate any switch. Leave the building and call the gas utility from outside.

How to clean a furnace flame sensor

The flame sensor is a thin metal rod in a white porcelain insulator, mounted so its tip sits in the burner flame. It has one wire and is usually held by one hex-head screw. On many furnaces it is at the opposite end of the burner row from the ignitor.

  1. With power and gas off, pull the single wire off the sensor terminal.
  2. Remove the mounting screw and slide the sensor straight out.
  3. Rub the metal rod lightly with a fine non-woven abrasive pad or fine steel wool until the dull film is gone. Do not use coarse sandpaper or emery cloth, which scratches the rod and leaves grit.
  4. Wipe the rod with a clean, dry cloth. Do not clean the porcelain with abrasives.
  5. Inspect the porcelain for cracks and the rod for pitting or bending. Replace the sensor if you find any.
  6. Reinstall the sensor in the same position and reconnect the wire.
  7. Restore gas and power and run a full cycle. The burners should stay lit.

If the burners still drop out, the cause may be a loose burner ground, a weak or lifting flame, or reversed polarity on the power supply. Those need a technician with a meter that reads flame current. A sensor that fouls again within weeks points to a combustion problem.

Can you clean a furnace ignitor?

No abrasive cleaning. The element of a hot surface ignitor is silicon carbide or silicon nitride. Skin oil, scratches and small knocks create weak spots that fail when the element heats. If the ignitor is dusty, leave it alone or blow the dust off gently without touching the element. If it is cracked, chalky white in one spot or does not glow, replace it.

How to test a furnace ignitor

  1. With the power off, unplug the ignitor from its harness and remove its mounting screw. Hold it by the ceramic base only.
  2. Look for hairline cracks, white spots and burned leads. A cracked ignitor must be replaced even if it still glows.
  3. Set a multimeter to ohms and touch the probes to the two connector pins.
  4. A reading of OL (open circuit) means the element is broken.
  5. If you get a reading, compare it with the manufacturer's specification for that ignitor. Resistance differs between silicon carbide and silicon nitride and between part numbers, so a general figure is not reliable.

If the ignitor tests good but never glows, the control may not be sending voltage. Measuring live 120 V inside a furnace is a job for a qualified technician.

How to choose a replacement ignitor or flame sensor

  • Start with the OEM part number. It is on the old part or in the furnace parts list. Ignitors that look alike differ in voltage, lead length, connector and bracket.
  • Match the voltage. Never fit an ignitor rated for a different voltage than the original.
  • Match the sensing type. Some universal ignitors are listed for remote flame sense applications only and must not be used on local sense systems. Check the ignitor instructions before ordering.
  • Universal kits. When the original is unavailable, a universal 120 V ignitor such as the Resideo Q3200U1004/U Glowfly can be used where its cross-reference lists your OEM part.
  • Flame sensors. Match rod length, bend and bracket. One example is the Resideo Q354A1018/U flame sensor.

Silicon carbide is the older grey element and is the more fragile of the two. Silicon nitride is tougher. Either is correct if the voltage, mounting and sensing type match the appliance.

What if the ignitor and sensor are both good?

The ignition control runs the light-off sequence and holds the safety timings. If the ignitor and sensor test good and the furnace still will not light, the control or the gas valve may be at fault. Replacement modules such as the Resideo S8910U3000/U universal hot surface ignition module must match the original's trial-for-ignition time, prepurge and flame sensing type. The trial time is a safety limit set by the appliance manufacturer: a replacement must never have a longer trial than the original. Diagnosis and replacement of a module or gas valve is technician work. See how to choose a furnace gas valve for the selection steps.

When to call a professional

  • You smell gas, or see soot, scorch marks or flame rolling out of the burner compartment.
  • The burners still drop out after the flame sensor is cleaned or replaced.
  • The ignitor glows but the burners never light.
  • A new ignitor fails again after a short time, which can indicate a voltage or control fault.
  • The work involves a gas valve, an ignition module, gas pressure or flame current measurement.
  • The furnace locks out repeatedly. Do not keep resetting it, and never jumper a pressure switch, rollout switch or limit switch to make it run.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a flame sensor be cleaned?

It is normally checked and cleaned during yearly furnace maintenance.

Can I clean a flame sensor with sandpaper?

Avoid coarse sandpaper. Use a fine non-woven abrasive pad or fine steel wool, then wipe the rod clean.

Why does my furnace light and then shut off?

The most common cause is a dirty or poorly grounded flame sensor. Other causes are a weak flame, a cracked sensor insulator, reversed supply polarity or a failing control.
