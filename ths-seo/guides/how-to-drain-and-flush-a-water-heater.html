To drain and flush a water heater, turn off the gas or the electricity, close the cold water supply, connect a garden hose to the drain valve, open a hot faucet, drain the tank, then flush it with short bursts of cold water until the water runs clear. Refill the tank completely before turning the power or gas back on. The job takes about an hour on a standard gas or electric tank water heater and removes the sediment that causes rumbling noises and slow recovery.

How to drain and flush a water heater: the short version

  1. Turn off the power (electric) or set the gas control to off or pilot (gas).
  2. Close the cold water supply valve to the heater.
  3. Connect a hose to the drain valve and run it to a drain or outdoors.
  4. Open a hot water faucet in the house.
  5. Open the drain valve and let the tank empty.
  6. Flush with bursts of cold water until the water runs clear.
  7. Close the drain valve and refill the tank with the hot faucet open.
  8. When water flows steadily from the faucet, restore power or gas.

Why flush a water heater, and how often?

Minerals in the water, mostly calcium and magnesium, settle on the bottom of the tank. On a gas heater the layer sits between the burner and the water, which causes popping or rumbling and slows recovery. On an electric heater it can bury the lower element. Most manufacturers' manuals call for draining and flushing at regular intervals; once a year is the common recommendation, and more often with hard water. Follow the interval in your heater's manual.

Signs that a flush is overdue: popping or rumbling while the heater runs, hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, or gritty water from the drain valve.

Should you flush an old water heater that has never been flushed?

Be cautious. On an old tank with heavy buildup, sediment can block the drain valve so that it will not close again, and disturbing the sediment can expose a tank that was already corroded. If the heater is near the end of its life, shows rust at the base or already weeps water, plan a replacement instead of a flush.

What you need

  • A garden hose that reaches a floor drain, a laundry sink or outdoors, to a point lower than the drain valve
  • A flat screwdriver or pliers, for drain valves with a slotted stem
  • Gloves and safety glasses
  • A non-contact voltage tester, for electric heaters

Safety before you start

  • Scalding. Water in the tank can be 120°F to 140°F or hotter. Shut the heater off and let the water cool for a few hours, or run hot water at a faucet until it cools, before draining. Make sure the hose end is secured and that the drain can accept hot water.
  • Electric heaters. Switch off the breaker, not only the thermostat. Electric water heaters run on 240 V. Confirm the power is off with a voltage tester before removing any cover.
  • Gas heaters. If you smell gas, stop, leave the building and call the gas utility from outside.

Step 1: Turn off the power or the gas

Electric: switch off the water heater breaker. This is the most important step in the job, for the reason given in Step 7.

Gas: turn the gas control knob to OFF, or to PILOT or VACATION if your heater's manual allows that setting for draining. The burner must not fire while the tank is empty. If the heater has a power vent blower or an electronic control, unplug it.

Step 2: Close the cold water supply

Close the shut-off valve on the cold water pipe entering the heater. The inlet is marked on the top of the tank.

Step 3: Connect the hose and open a hot faucet

Thread the hose onto the drain valve near the bottom of the tank and run it to the drain. Then open a hot water faucet, preferably on a floor above the heater. This lets air into the tank so it drains instead of holding a vacuum.

Step 4: Drain the tank

Open the drain valve slowly. The water is often cloudy at first and may carry sand-like particles. A 40 or 50 gallon tank takes roughly 20 minutes to an hour, depending on the valve and the sediment.

If the flow stops while the tank is still full, sediment has blocked the valve. Open the cold supply for a few seconds to push it through. Do not force a stiff plastic drain valve, because it can crack. A valve that will not seal afterward can be replaced with a brass boiler drain of the same thread size once the tank is empty.

Step 5: Flush the sediment

  1. With the drain valve open, open the cold supply for 30 to 60 seconds. The incoming water stirs the sediment and carries it out.
  2. Close the cold supply and let the tank drain.
  3. Repeat until the water from the hose is clear.

Step 6: Close the drain and refill

  1. Close the drain valve and remove the hose.
  2. Leave the hot faucet open and open the cold supply fully.
  3. Wait until water flows from the faucet in a steady stream with no sputtering. That means the tank is full and the air is out.
  4. Close the faucet, then check the drain valve for drips.

Step 7: Restore power or gas only when the tank is full

Electric: never switch the breaker on until the tank is full. Heating elements are designed to work under water. An element that is energized in air overheats and burns out within seconds, which is called dry firing. If that happens, replacements are in water heater elements; match the voltage, wattage and mounting style on the old element.

Gas: turn the control back to ON and to your normal temperature setting. If the pilot went out, relight it by following the lighting instructions on the heater's label exactly. Plug in the blower or control if you unplugged it. Allow about an hour for the tank to reheat.

What about the T&P relief valve?

The temperature and pressure (T&P) relief valve on the top or side of the tank opens if the temperature or pressure becomes dangerous. Three rules apply:

  • Do not use it to vent the tank while draining. A hot faucet does the same job, and an old valve that is lifted may not reseat.
  • If you test it as the manufacturer recommends, stand clear of the discharge pipe. The water is scalding.
  • Never cap, plug or remove the valve or its discharge pipe. A valve that drips or will not reseat must be replaced with one that matches the pressure and BTU/h ratings on the heater's label. See relief valves.

A relief valve that discharges on its own often indicates excess pressure from thermal expansion, which a water heater expansion tank is designed to absorb. Have a plumber confirm the cause.

What else should you check while you are there?

  • Anode rod. The anode corrodes in place of the steel tank. If it is worn down to its core wire, replace it. See dip tubes and anode rods.
  • Leaks. Water from a fitting or valve can be repaired. Water from the tank shell itself means the heater needs replacing.

When to call a professional

  • You smell gas, or the pilot or burner will not relight normally after the flush.
  • The drain valve is blocked or will not close, and you are not able to replace it.
  • The T&P relief valve leaks, discharges or needs replacing.
  • The tank itself leaks, or still rumbles after a thorough flush.
  • You are not comfortable working around a 240 V circuit or a gas control.

If the tank is leaking or past its service life, compare replacements in residential gas tank water heaters, and see what size water heater you need.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to turn off the water heater to flush it?

Yes. An electric element that is powered without water burns out, and a gas burner must not fire into an empty tank.

Does this apply to tankless water heaters?

No. Tankless heaters are descaled by circulating a cleaning solution through service valves, following the manufacturer's procedure.
