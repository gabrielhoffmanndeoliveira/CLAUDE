Radiators that are cold at the top, baseboard that gurgles, a boiler that keeps losing or gaining pressure: these are the most common hot water heating complaints, and most of them come down to air in the system or a pressure problem. This guide shows how to bleed a radiator and baseboard, how to check and restore boiler pressure, and what causes boiler pressure to run too high.

Note: this guide covers hot water (hydronic) systems. Steam radiators work differently: their air vents release air automatically while the system runs, and they should not be bled like a hot water radiator.

Why air gets into a heating system

A hot water heating system is a closed loop, but air still finds its way in:

  • Fresh water carries dissolved air. Every time the system is filled or topped up, new water brings oxygen and other gases that come out of solution as the water heats.
  • Repairs and draining. Replacing a circulator, zone valve or radiator lets air into the piping.
  • Small leaks and low pressure. If pressure at the highest point drops too low, air can be drawn in through vents, valve packing or fittings.
  • Corrosion. Chemical reactions inside steel and iron components can create gas over time.

Air rises to the high points: the top of radiators, the ends of baseboard runs and upper-floor piping. It blocks flow, so the top of a radiator stays cool, and it causes gurgling or "running water" noises. Air also speeds up corrosion and can damage the circulator.

Tools you need

  • Radiator key or screwdriver. Most manual radiator vents have a small square stem that takes a radiator key, such as the Pasco 606 radiator key. Others have a slot for a flat screwdriver or a coin. Keep a spare key with the boiler.
  • A rag and a small cup or bucket to catch water.
  • A garden hose and bucket for purging baseboard zones.
  • A pressure gauge you can trust. If the boiler gauge is stuck or unreadable, a combination temperature and pressure gauge such as the Resideo TD-165 Tridicator or Legend 800-673 is an easy replacement.

How to bleed a radiator, step by step

  1. Turn the heat up first, then off. Run the heat until the radiators are warm so you can find the cold spots. Then set the thermostat down and turn off the circulator (or the boiler) so water is not moving while you bleed. Let the radiators cool enough to touch. Hot water can scald.
  2. Check the boiler pressure. Note the reading on the gauge before you start. You need positive pressure to push the air out.
  3. Start low, work high. A common approach is to begin on the lowest floor and work up, starting with the radiator farthest from the boiler on each floor.
  4. Find the bleed valve. It is a small brass or chrome fitting near the top of the radiator, on the end opposite the supply valve.
  5. Open it slowly. Hold a rag and cup under the vent and turn the key a quarter to half turn counterclockwise. You will hear air hiss. Do not unscrew the stem all the way.
  6. Close it when water comes out. Once a steady stream of water with no sputtering appears, close the vent snugly. Do not overtighten.
  7. Repeat on every radiator that has a vent, then go back to the first one and check again.
  8. Re-check the boiler pressure and top up if needed (see below). Turn the heat back on and confirm the radiators heat evenly.

If a radiator vent is corroded or keeps leaking, replace it. A coin-operated vent such as the Taco 417-3 coin vent (1/8" NPT) is a common upgrade. Match the thread size before ordering.

How to bleed baseboard heat

Most fin-tube baseboard has no bleed valve on each run. Some systems have a small manual vent at the end of a run, under the cover. If yours does, bleed it like a radiator. If not, you remove air by purging each zone through a purge valve near the boiler:

  1. Turn off the boiler and let the water cool.
  2. Find the zone's purge valve. It is usually a boiler drain or a ball valve with a hose thread on the return pipe of each zone, next to a shut-off valve.
  3. Close the zone's shut-off valve between the purge valve and the boiler, so water has to travel through the whole zone before it comes out.
  4. Connect a hose from the purge valve to a bucket or floor drain and open the purge valve.
  5. Feed fresh water in. Lift the fast-fill lever on the boiler fill valve (or open the bypass) so water pushes through the zone. Watch the boiler pressure gauge and do not let it approach the relief valve setting.
  6. Run until the air stops. When the hose runs solid with no bursts of air, close the purge valve, then release the fast-fill lever.
  7. Repeat for each zone, reopen all shut-off valves, set the cold pressure and restart the boiler.

Zones with zone valves may need the valve opened manually during purging. Check the manual lever on the zone valve or see our zone valve guide.

Stop bleeding by hand: automatic air vents

Automatic air vents release air on their own at the system's high points, at air separators and on air scoops. A small float inside opens the vent when air collects and closes it when water returns.

  • Taco 418-4 Hy-Vent (1/2" x 3/4" NPT) and Taco 400-4 Hy-Vent (1/8" NPT) for high points and air scoops.
  • Caleffi MAXCAL 501502A (3/4" NPT female), a larger-capacity vent.
  • Caleffi Robocal 502710A (1/8" male, with check valve), a compact vent for manifolds and terminal units. The check valve lets you remove it without draining the system.
  • Resideo SuperVent SV175 (1/2", with check valve).

Checking and restoring boiler pressure

The pressure gauge on the boiler (often combined with a thermometer) shows the pressure of the heating loop. Read it with the system cold and again when it is hot.

  • Cold: many residential systems are filled to about 12 to 15 psi. That is enough to lift water to the top of a typical two-story home with a few psi to spare at the highest radiator.
  • Hot: pressure rises as the water expands. Readings of roughly 18 to 25 psi are common in a hot residential system.
  • Relief valve: most residential boilers have a relief valve set at 30 psi.

These are general figures. Your boiler manual gives the correct range for your system, and taller homes need a higher fill pressure.

How to top up the pressure

After bleeding, pressure usually drops. Most systems refill through a boiler fill valve (also called a feed valve or pressure reducing valve) on the cold water line:

  • On an automatic fill valve such as the Taco 329-3, Resideo FM911 or Caleffi 573012A COMBO AutoFill, make sure the inlet valve is open. The valve should bring the system up to its set pressure on its own.
  • If the system is still low, lift the fast-fill lever briefly while watching the gauge, then release it.
  • Many combi boilers have a built-in fill valve or filling loop. Follow the manual. See our combi boiler guide for more on combi maintenance.

If you have to top up often, there is a leak somewhere: at a radiator valve, a fitting, a relief valve that drips, or inside the boiler. Find and fix it, because constant fresh water brings in more air and minerals.

Boiler pressure too high? Common causes

If the gauge climbs well above the normal hot range, or approaches 30 psi, look at these causes in order:

1. Failed or waterlogged expansion tank

The expansion tank gives heated water room to expand. Inside, a diaphragm or bladder separates the system water from a cushion of air. When the air charge leaks away or the bladder fails, the tank fills with water and can no longer absorb expansion, so pressure spikes every time the boiler fires.

  • Quick checks: tap the tank. A good tank sounds hollow at the top. Press the Schrader (tire) valve briefly: water coming out means the bladder has failed.
  • Pre-charge: the air charge should be checked with the tank isolated from system pressure and is usually set to match the cold fill pressure. Follow the tank manufacturer's instructions.
  • Replacement: heating tanks such as the Amtrol EX-15 and EX-30 Extrol are common in homes. Size by system water volume. See all heating expansion tanks, and read our guide Expansion Tanks: What They Do and What Size You Need.

Older systems may use a steel compression tank without a bladder. These slowly waterlog and need to be drained to restore the air cushion.

2. Leaking fill valve (pressure reducing valve)

If pressure creeps up even when the boiler is cold or off, the fill valve may be passing water from the higher-pressure domestic supply. Close the valve on the cold water feed for a day. If the creep stops, the fill valve is the culprit. Some valves can be rebuilt (Caleffi offers an AutoFill replacement cartridge); otherwise replace the valve. Browse boiler fill and feed valves.

3. Overfilling

Holding the fast-fill lever too long, or setting the fill valve too high, leaves the system with too much cold pressure. Drain a little water from a boiler drain with the system cold until you reach the correct cold pressure.

4. A leaking indirect water heater coil

If you have an indirect water heater, a leak in its internal coil can push domestic water (at street pressure) into the boiler loop. If pressure keeps rising and the fill valve is shut, have a professional test the coil.

When the relief valve discharges, call a pro

The boiler relief valve is a safety device. If it releases water, the system reached its set pressure (usually 30 psi) or the valve itself has failed. Do not plug, cap or remove the discharge pipe, and never replace a relief valve with one rated higher than the boiler allows.

A relief valve that opens is a warning sign. Turn the boiler off and have a licensed heating professional find the cause. Old relief valves can also leak from mineral buildup. A professional can replace one with the correct pressure rating and capacity, such as the Watts 0274428 (3/4", 30 psi). See all relief valves.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my radiator needs bleeding?

The top of the radiator is cool while the bottom is warm, or you hear gurgling. A radiator that is cold all over usually has a different problem, such as a closed valve, a stuck zone valve or no flow.

Should the heating be on or off when bleeding radiators?

Run it first to find the cold radiators, then turn it off and let the system cool before opening any vent. This reduces the risk of burns and lets air settle at the top.

What should my boiler pressure be?

Many residential systems run about 12 to 15 psi cold and roughly 18 to 25 psi hot, with a 30 psi relief valve. Your boiler manual has the correct range for your home.

Why does my boiler pressure go up when the heat is on?

Some rise is normal because water expands as it heats. A large jump, especially toward 30 psi, usually means the expansion tank has lost its air charge or failed.

Why does my boiler keep losing pressure?

Bleeding releases a little pressure, which is normal. Ongoing loss means a leak or a relief valve that drips. Check radiator valves, fittings and the relief valve discharge pipe.
