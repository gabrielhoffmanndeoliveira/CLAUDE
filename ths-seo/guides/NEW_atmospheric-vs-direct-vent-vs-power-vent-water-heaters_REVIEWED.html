Gas tank water heaters come in four vent types, and the difference is where the combustion air comes from and what moves the exhaust out. An atmospheric heater takes air from the room and lets hot exhaust rise up a metal flue on its own. A direct vent heater is sealed from the room, takes air from outdoors and exhausts through the same wall, with no fan. A power vent heater takes air from the room and uses an electric blower to push exhaust out through plastic pipe. A power direct vent heater does both: outdoor air in, blower-driven exhaust out. A replacement normally has to be the same type as the heater it replaces, because the vent system in the house was built for that type.

How to tell which one you have

You can identify the type in a minute by looking at the top of the heater and following the pipe.

  1. Look at the top center of the tank.
    • A metal cone or cap standing on short legs, with an open gap between it and the tank, is a draft hood. That is an atmospheric heater.
    • A plastic or metal box with a motor and an electrical cord is a blower. That is a power vent or power direct vent heater.
    • A sealed metal collar with no gap and no blower, connected to a pipe that goes straight out the wall, is a direct vent heater.
  2. Look at the vent pipe material. Metal pipe rising toward the roof or into a chimney means atmospheric. White, gray or black plastic pipe means a blower-driven model.
  3. Count the pipes. One plastic pipe from the blower is power vent. Two plastic pipes (or one concentric termination outside with two pipes inside) is power direct vent.
  4. Check for a power cord. Power vent and power direct vent models plug into a receptacle. Most atmospheric and direct vent models have no cord.
  5. Read the rating plate. The model number often carries the vent type. On Rheem Professional Classic models, for example, the suffix -PV marks power vent, -PDV power direct vent and -DV direct vent; models without a vent suffix are atmospheric. The manual for the model number confirms it.

Atmospheric vent

This is the traditional design. The burner draws air from the space around the heater, and the exhaust rises through a central flue, into the draft hood, and up the vent by buoyancy. The draft hood deliberately lets room air mix into the vent to keep the draft steady.

  • Needs: a vertical vent that ends above the roof, correctly sized, and enough combustion air in the room. The fuel gas code requires vent connectors to slope upward at least 1/4 inch per foot.
  • Strengths: simple, no fan, and most models work during a power outage.
  • Limits: it cannot vent sideways through a wall, and it can backdraft (spill exhaust into the room) if the vent is blocked, undersized, oversized, or if exhaust fans or a tight house pull the room pressure down.

One situation to know: when a furnace that shared the chimney is replaced with a high-efficiency model vented through the wall, the water heater is left alone on a chimney that is now too large for it. The chimney usually needs a liner sized for the water heater.

Direct vent

A direct vent heater has a sealed combustion chamber. Air comes in from outdoors and exhaust goes out through the same wall penetration, typically through a coaxial pipe with exhaust in the center and incoming air around it. No fan is involved, so vent runs are short and the heater has to sit on or near an outside wall.

  • Needs: the manufacturer's vent kit and a location within the kit's maximum length.
  • Strengths: it does not use indoor air, so it suits tight homes, closets and rooms where depressurization is a concern.
  • Limits: placement is restricted by the short vent length.

Power vent

A blower on top of the heater pulls exhaust through the flue, mixes it with room air to cool it, and pushes it out through plastic pipe. Because the fan does the work, the vent can run horizontally to a side wall and over much longer distances than a direct vent.

  • Needs: a 120 V receptacle, the pipe material and diameter the manual specifies, and enough combustion air in the room.
  • Strengths: no chimney required, flexible placement.
  • Limits: no hot water recovery during a power outage, some fan noise, and more parts (blower, pressure switch, control).

Power direct vent

This type combines the two ideas: a sealed combustion chamber fed with outdoor air through one pipe, and a blower that pushes exhaust out through another. Rheem describes its power direct vent models as using sealed combustion with a blower moving both intake air and exhaust, and lists PVC, ABS or CPVC vent and air inlet pipe on its Professional Classic PDV models. Check the manual of the specific model for its limits.

  • Strengths: independent of indoor air and flexible on location.
  • Limits: two pipes to route, and it needs power.

Why the replacement has to match

The vent in your house is part of the appliance's listing. Crossing types creates specific hazards:

  • A power vent heater must not be connected to a metal B-vent or chimney. Its vent runs under positive pressure and B-vent joints are not gas-tight.
  • An atmospheric heater must not be connected to plastic pipe. Its exhaust is far too hot for PVC.
  • An atmospheric heater cannot be vented horizontally through a wall.
  • Even within one type, diameter, maximum length and termination kit are specific to the model. A new power vent heater may not be approved for the pipe size left by the old one.

Changing type is possible, for example going from atmospheric to power vent when a chimney is being removed, but it means a new vent route, an electrical outlet and often a permit review. It is a project, not a like-for-like swap.

Buying checklist

  1. Identify the vent type and write down the model number from the rating plate.
  2. Confirm the fuel: natural gas or propane.
  3. Measure the vent diameter and, for plastic vents, the total length and number of elbows.
  4. Measure the space: height to the vent connection, diameter, and clearances.
  5. Match capacity and first-hour rating to the household. See what size water heater do I need.
  6. Compare the new model's vent table with what is installed.

When to call a professional

Replacing a gas water heater involves the gas connection, the vent, the temperature and pressure relief valve and, on powered models, the electrical supply. Mistakes in gas piping or venting can cause fire, explosion or carbon monoxide poisoning. Most jurisdictions require a permit for water heater replacement, and many require a licensed plumber or gas fitter for the gas connection. Call a licensed professional if you smell gas, if you see soot, scorching or melted plastic around the draft hood, if a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, if the vent type has to change, or if you are not certain the existing vent is correct for the new heater. Install carbon monoxide alarms as your local code requires.
