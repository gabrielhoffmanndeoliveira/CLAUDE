PEX-A and PEX-B are both cross-linked polyethylene water tubing made to the same standard (ASTM F876) with the same pressure ratings. The difference is how they are cross-linked, and that changes how they handle. PEX-A is more flexible, can be joined with cold expansion fittings (ASTM F1960) and can be repaired with a heat gun if it kinks. PEX-B is stiffer, usually costs less, and is joined with crimp or clamp fittings. The letters are not quality grades: A, B and C only identify the manufacturing method.

What do the letters A, B and C mean?

Plain polyethylene softens with heat. Cross-linking bonds its long molecules into a network, which gives PEX its resistance to heat, pressure and creep. The letter tells you which process made those bonds:

  • PEX-A (peroxide or Engel method): peroxide is mixed into the polyethylene and the cross-linking takes place while the material is hot, during extrusion.
  • PEX-B (silane method): a silane compound is grafted onto the polyethylene, the tubing is extruded, and cross-linking is completed afterward with heat and moisture.
  • PEX-C (irradiation method): the extruded tubing is cross-linked with an electron beam. It is joined with crimp or clamp fittings.

ASTM F876 requires every PEX tube, whatever the method, to fall within a set range of cross-linking (65 to 89 percent) and to pass the same pressure, temperature and chlorine resistance tests. PEX-A is typically at the upper part of that range and PEX-B at the lower part. A higher degree of cross-linking does not mean a higher pressure rating.

Which is more flexible?

PEX-A. It uncoils more easily, bends tighter and is easier to work in cold weather and tight framing. Tighter bends mean fewer elbows. PEX-B holds more of its coil shape, needs a larger bend radius and uses bend supports or elbow fittings more often. Its stiffness can be an advantage on long straight runs.

The minimum bend radius is set by the tubing manufacturer. Bending tighter than that can kink the tubing.

Expansion fittings (F1960)

Expansion joints depend on the tubing shrinking back to its original size, which PEX-A does reliably. Because the tube is stretched over the fitting instead of the fitting being inserted into an unstretched tube, an F1960 fitting has a larger bore than an insert fitting of the same nominal size and restricts flow less. In cold conditions the joint takes longer to shrink, so wait before pressure testing.

Use F1960 fittings only on tubing whose print line lists F1960. Some manufacturers make expansion-capable PEX-B with their own rings and fittings. Do not mix components between systems unless the manufacturers list them together. See F1960 expansion fittings and PEX expansion tools.

Crimp and clamp fittings (F1807, F2159)

Crimp and clamp fittings work on PEX-B and PEX-C and on PEX-A that is listed for them. Crimp tools are sized to the ring and each joint is checked with a go/no-go gauge. Clamp tools usually cover several sizes with one tool. See PEX crimp fittings (F1807), all PEX fittings and PEX tools.

Can a kink in PEX be repaired?

  • PEX-A: manufacturers of PEX-A allow a kink to be repaired by warming the section evenly with a heat gun until the tubing returns to its round shape. Follow the manufacturer's procedure, never use an open flame, and do not repair tubing that is under pressure.
  • PEX-B: a kink cannot be heat-repaired. Cut it out and install a coupling.

How do the costs compare?

  • Tubing: PEX-B generally costs less per foot.
  • Tools: crimp and clamp tools cost much less than an expansion tool, especially a battery-powered one. For a small repair, tool cost often decides the choice.
  • Whole-house work: PEX-A's flexibility reduces the number of fittings, which offsets part of the material cost on a repipe.

What applies to both types?

  • Read the print line. It lists the standards, pressure ratings and fitting systems the tubing is certified for. Potable water tubing carries NSF/ANSI/CAN 61.
  • Sunlight. PEX is not for permanent outdoor exposure. Each product has a maximum UV exposure time, so store it covered.
  • Heat. Keep PEX away from flues and recessed lights and follow the code and manufacturer's minimum distance at water heater connections.
  • Heating systems. Hydronic heating with ferrous components needs PEX with an oxygen barrier. Standard potable PEX of either type does not have one.
  • Freezing. PEX tolerates freezing better than rigid pipe but can still split, and fittings can fail. Protect lines in unheated spaces.

Which should you choose?

  • Choose PEX-A for a repipe or a large project, when you want expansion fittings and the tightest bends, or when you already own an expansion tool.
  • Choose PEX-B for repairs and smaller projects, when you want lower tubing and tool cost, or when you already own a crimp or clamp tool.
  • Either way, stay with one connection system for the whole job and use tubing, fittings and rings that are listed together.

When to call a professional

  • A whole-house repipe or new supply lines. These usually need a permit, a pressure test and an inspection before walls are closed.
  • Connections at a water heater or boiler.
  • Replacing copper that is part of the electrical bonding path. An electrician should check whether a bonding jumper is needed.
  • Any installation where you cannot confirm from the print line that the tubing and fittings are listed together.

Frequently asked questions

Is PEX-A better than PEX-B?

Not in rating. Both meet ASTM F876 and carry the same pressure ratings. PEX-A is easier to bend and allows expansion fittings; PEX-B costs less.

Can I use crimp rings on PEX-A?

Yes, if the tubing's print line lists ASTM F1807 or F2159.

Can I use expansion fittings on PEX-B?

Only on PEX-B that its manufacturer lists for F1960 expansion, with the rings that manufacturer specifies. Standard PEX-B is not suitable.

Can PEX-A and PEX-B be joined together?

Yes, with a coupling whose fitting system is listed for both tubes, such as a crimp or push-to-connect coupling.
