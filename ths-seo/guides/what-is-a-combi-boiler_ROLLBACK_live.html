A combi boiler is one wall-hung appliance that does two jobs: it heats your home through radiators, baseboard or radiant floors, and it makes domestic hot water on demand for showers and taps, with no storage tank. Combis are popular in smaller homes and tight mechanical rooms, but they are not the right fit for every house. This guide explains how a combi works, how to size one, when a boiler plus an indirect tank is the better choice, and what venting, gas piping and maintenance involve.

What is a combi boiler?

"Combi" is short for combination boiler. Inside one cabinet you get a gas burner, a primary heat exchanger, a circulator, a diverter (3-way) valve, a small plate heat exchanger for domestic hot water and the controls that run it all. Most combis sold in the US today are condensing, high-efficiency units with sealed combustion.

A standard heating-only boiler, by contrast, makes hot water for the heating system only. If you also want stored hot water from it, you add a separate indirect water heater (an insulated tank with a coil that the boiler heats as its own zone).

How a combi heats your home and your water

A combi gives domestic hot water (DHW) priority. It switches between two modes:

  • Space heating mode. When a thermostat calls for heat, the burner fires and the internal pump circulates boiler water through the heating loop, just like a regular boiler. Modulating burners vary their output to match the load.
  • Hot water mode. When you open a hot tap, a flow sensor detects the draw. The diverter valve sends boiler water through the plate heat exchanger instead of the heating loop, and the burner ramps up to heat the cold water passing through the other side. Close the tap and the boiler returns to heating.

During a long shower the house gets no new heat, but a properly sized system rarely notices because radiators and floors hold heat. The domestic side works much like a gas tankless water heater. If you want the background on that, see how a tankless water heater works.

Combi boiler vs tankless water heater

People often compare a combi boiler to a tankless water heater because both make hot water on demand. The difference is what else they do:

Combi boiler Tankless water heater
Space heating Yes, through a hydronic system (radiators, baseboard, radiant) No (some can serve a fan coil or air handler with the right kit and approvals)
Domestic hot water On demand, with DHW priority On demand
Closed heating loop Yes: needs expansion tank, relief valve, fill valve and air removal No
Best for Homes with hydronic heat and moderate hot water demand Homes heated by a furnace or heat pump

If your house is heated by forced air, a tankless water heater is usually the simpler upgrade. If it has radiators or baseboard and an old boiler plus a tank-type water heater, a combi can replace both. For tankless sizing basics, read what size tankless water heater you need. How to size a combi boiler

A combi has two ratings, and you need to check both. Most sizing mistakes come from looking at only one.

1. Heating load (BTU/h)

The heating side should match the home's design heat loss, ideally from a room-by-room heat loss calculation (ACCA Manual J or a similar method). The nameplate on your old boiler is not a reliable guide, because many older boilers are oversized.

Turndown matters too. The Navien NCB-240/130H lists a heating input range of 13,000 to 130,000 BTU/h, and the IBC CX199 modulates from 20,000 to 199,000 BTU/h (10:1 turndown). A low minimum input lets the boiler run long, efficient cycles on mild days instead of short-cycling.

2. Hot water demand (GPM)

The domestic side is sized by flow rate in gallons per minute (GPM) at your temperature rise. Temperature rise is the difference between your incoming cold water and the hot water temperature you want, for example 50°F in, 120°F out = 70°F rise. A useful rule of thumb:

GPM ≈ BTU/h output ÷ (500 × temperature rise in °F)

A combi with about 190,000 BTU/h of DHW output at a 70°F rise gives roughly 5.4 GPM. That lines up with the NCB-240/130H, which lists 5.1 GPM at a 70°F rise from a 199,900 BTU/h DHW input. Add up the fixtures you will run at the same time (a showerhead is limited to 2.5 GPM by federal standards, and many use less) and compare. In cold-water regions your rise is larger, so the same unit delivers fewer GPM in winter.

Where the models fit

  • Smaller heat loads: Navien NCB-240/110H (110,000 BTU/h heating, 199,900 BTU/h DHW) and Lochinvar Noble NKC110N (110 MBH, 95% AFUE, natural gas).
  • Mid-size homes: Navien NCB-240/130H and IBC SFC160-1 (160,000 BTU/h input).
  • Larger heat loads: Navien NFC-250/175H and NFC-250/200H (210,000 BTU/h DHW input), IBC CX199, Noritz NRCB199DVNG and the US Boiler Alta ALTAC-200 (200,000 BTU/h input, pump included).

Always confirm the final choice with the manufacturer's sizing tables and your installer.

When a combi fits, and when to choose a boiler + indirect tank

A combi is usually a good fit when

  • The home has one to two (sometimes two and a half) bathrooms and rarely runs more than two hot fixtures at once.
  • Space is tight: a wall-hung combi frees the floor space of a boiler and a water heater.
  • The heat load is moderate and the house already has hydronic heat.
  • You want one appliance, one vent and one gas connection to service.

A boiler plus an indirect water heater is often better when

  • Several showers, a large soaking tub or a body-spray shower run at the same time. A tank stores a reserve that covers peaks a combi cannot.
  • Your incoming water is very cold or very hard. Hard water scales plate heat exchangers quickly.
  • The heat load is large, or you need many heating zones, and you would rather size the boiler to the heat loss alone.
  • You want hot water recirculation with minimal wait, which a storage tank handles more easily.

Combi boiler pros and cons

Pros: endless hot water within the unit's GPM limit, no standby tank losses, compact wall-hung design, high efficiency with modulation, and one appliance instead of two.

Cons: hot water flow is limited (a demanding household may notice), heating pauses while hot water runs, plate heat exchangers need descaling in hard water, and more components sit in one box, so a failure can take out both heat and hot water.

Venting a condensing combi (PVC, CPVC or polypropylene)

Condensing combis produce cool, acidic flue gas and condensate, so they vent through plastic or stainless pipe instead of a masonry chimney. Typical options are PVC, CPVC, polypropylene (PP) or stainless steel, as a two-pipe direct vent or a concentric kit. What is allowed depends on the model:

  • The Navien NCB-240/130H lists 2" or 3" venting in polypropylene, PVC, CPVC or stainless.
  • The IBC CX199 calls for 3" PVC, CPVC or PPs flue and intake, up to 150 ft equivalent length, and does not permit 2" PPs.

Maximum vent length shrinks with each elbow, and some manufacturers require CPVC or PP for the first section near the boiler. Follow the installation manual exactly, keep the terminals clear of windows, doors and snow lines, and run the condensate to a drain through a condensate neutralizer where required. Gas line sizing: an important caveat

A combi can fire at around 200,000 BTU/h when making hot water. That is often more than double the old boiler or water heater it replaces, and the existing gas line may be too small. An undersized line can cause poor hot water performance, ignition faults and error codes.

  • A 3/4" gas connection on the appliance (as on the NCB-240/130H) does not mean a 3/4" line is enough. Pipe size depends on total connected load, pipe length and material, and your gas pressure.
  • Check the supply pressure range in the manual. The IBC CX199, for example, lists a 1/2" NPT gas inlet and 4 to 14 in. w.c. supply pressure.
  • Your gas meter and regulator must handle the added load.

Safety note: gas piping, venting and combustion setup should be done by a licensed installer following the manufacturer's manual, the fuel gas code and local requirements. A combustion analysis at startup is the only way to confirm safe, efficient operation.

Combi boiler maintenance

  • Check system pressure. The heating loop is a closed system. Look at the gauge or display when the system is cold and compare with the manual's range. Frequent pressure loss means a leak; rising pressure points to the expansion tank or fill valve. Our guide on how to bleed a radiator and fix high boiler pressure covers both.
  • Flush the DHW heat exchanger. In hard water areas, descale the plate heat exchanger on the schedule in the manual, using isolation valves and a pump-and-bucket flush kit as you would on a tankless heater. Tankless valve kits make this easier on the domestic side.
  • Clean the filters. Clean the cold water inlet filter and any heating return filter. A magnetic boiler filter such as the Navien NaviClean helps catch magnetite before it reaches the heat exchanger.
  • Inspect the condensate trap and vent. Make sure the trap is clean and full, and that the vent and intake terminals are clear.
  • Annual service. Have a professional inspect the burner, check combustion, test the relief valve and verify the expansion tank pre-charge once a year.
  • Learn the error codes. Navien owners can start with our Navien error codes guide. Many codes, such as ignition or flow faults, overlap with Navien combi models. Always confirm in your model's manual.

Frequently asked questions

What is a combi boiler in simple terms?

It is a boiler that heats your home and also heats your tap water instantly, without a storage tank. One appliance replaces a heating boiler and a water heater.

Is a combi boiler the same as a tankless water heater?

No. Both heat water on demand, but a combi also runs your hydronic heating system. A tankless water heater only makes domestic hot water.

Can a combi boiler run two showers at once?

Often yes, if the unit's GPM at your temperature rise covers both showerheads. With very cold incoming water or high-flow fixtures, a boiler with an indirect tank is the safer choice.

Do I need an expansion tank with a combi boiler?

The heating loop always needs expansion capacity. Some combis include an internal expansion vessel, others do not, and the built-in vessel may be too small for larger systems. Check the manual and size an external heating expansion tank if needed.

How long does a combi boiler last?

Lifespan depends on water quality, installation and maintenance. Annual service, clean system water and regular descaling in hard water areas make the biggest difference. Check the manufacturer's warranty terms for your model.
