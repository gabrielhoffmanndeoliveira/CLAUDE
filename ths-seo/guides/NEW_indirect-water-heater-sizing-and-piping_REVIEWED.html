An indirect water heater is sized by two numbers together: the tank volume, which covers short peaks such as back-to-back showers, and the boiler output available to it, which sets how fast it recovers. A 40 to 50 gallon indirect on a boiler that can deliver 100,000 BTU per hour or more to it serves most households of three to five people. The tank has no burner. It connects to the boiler as a separate heating zone: boiler water is pumped through a coil inside the tank, and a control gives that zone priority whenever the tank calls for heat.

How an indirect water heater works

The tank holds domestic water. Inside it is a heat exchanger, usually a coil of smooth or corrugated tube, though some designs use a tank within a tank. Hot boiler water flows through the coil and heats the domestic water around it. The two never mix. An aquastat or temperature sensor in the tank tells the boiler when to fire and the circulator when to run.

Because the heat comes from a boiler that is typically several times more powerful than the burner of a standard tank water heater, recovery is fast, and the well-insulated tank loses little heat between draws.

Step 1: Estimate peak demand

Add up the hot water used in the busiest hour of a normal day. Typical figures at the tap: a shower uses about 2 gallons of mixed water per minute, of which roughly two thirds is hot; a bath 15 to 25 gallons of hot water; a dishwasher or clothes washer load 5 to 15 gallons depending on the machine. Three ten-minute showers and a dishwasher load in one hour comes to about 50 gallons of hot water.

Step 2: Understand first-hour rating and its conditions

Manufacturers publish a first-hour rating (gallons delivered in the first hour starting with a full hot tank) and a continuous rating (gallons per hour after that). Both are stated at specific conditions, normally 180°F boiler supply water, a given flow through the coil in gallons per minute, and a required boiler output in BTU per hour. The exact test conditions differ by manufacturer, so compare data sheets carefully.

The published rating applies only if your boiler meets those conditions. If the boiler is smaller than the output listed, recovery is limited by the boiler, not by the tank. A quick check:

Continuous recovery (gallons per hour) = boiler output to the tank (BTU/h) ÷ (8.33 × temperature rise in °F)

These are upper limits assuming the coil can transfer all the boiler's output. A small coil, low flow or lower boiler water temperature reduces them. A 70°F rise takes 50°F water to 120°F; a 90°F rise takes it to 140°F.

Step 3: Choose the tank size

HouseholdTypical indirect tank size
1 to 2 people, one bathroom30 to 40 gallons
3 to 4 people, two bathrooms40 to 50 gallons
5 or more people, three bathrooms60 to 80 gallons
Large soaking tub, multi-head shower, or small boiler80 gallons and up

Use this as a starting point and adjust for two things:

  • Small or modulating boilers. If the boiler was sized to a modest heat loss (for example 50,000 to 80,000 BTU/h), choose a larger tank so stored volume covers the peak while the boiler catches up.
  • Large dump loads. A big tub empties a tank faster than any residential boiler can refill it. Size the tank to the tub: the stored hot water, mixed down to bathing temperature, should fill it.

Storing water at 140°F and mixing it down to 120°F at the outlet increases the usable hot water from a given tank by roughly a quarter to a third, depending on the cold water temperature. That requires a thermostatic mixing valve, covered below.

How it connects to the boiler

The boiler side has two connections on the tank: coil supply and coil return. The domestic side has cold in and hot out, plus a relief valve port.

  1. Pipe the tank as its own zone. Use a dedicated circulator or, on zone valve systems, a full-port zone valve sized for the coil flow. On condensing boilers with primary/secondary piping, many manufacturers show the indirect connected directly to the boiler loop so it can receive hotter water than the heating zones.
  2. Use full-size pipe to the coil. Many residential indirect coils have 1 inch connections. Use the pipe size the tank manual specifies; reducing it cuts flow and recovery.
  3. Size the circulator to the coil. The data sheet gives the coil's pressure drop at its rated flow. The circulator has to deliver that flow against the combined head of coil, piping and boiler.
  4. Install a flow check (or a circulator with an integral check) so hot boiler water does not drift through the coil by gravity when the zone is off, and tank heat does not drift back into the heating system.
  5. Wire the tank control. The aquastat or sensor connects to the boiler's DHW input or to a zone relay with a priority setting.
  6. Set domestic hot water priority. During a tank call the control pauses space heating so the full boiler output goes to the tank. Tank calls are short, so rooms rarely cool noticeably. Most controls include a time limit that returns heat to the house if the call runs long.
  7. Set the boiler temperature for DHW calls. A condensing boiler running low-temperature heating on outdoor reset needs to target a higher supply temperature when the tank calls. Most have a separate DHW setpoint.
  8. Purge air from the coil circuit through a purge valve before putting it into service.

Domestic side requirements

  • Temperature and pressure relief valve. The tank needs its own listed T&P relief valve in the marked port, with a discharge pipe run full size to a safe location, never capped or reduced. See T&P relief valves.
  • Thermal expansion. If the cold supply has a check valve, backflow preventer or pressure reducing valve, a potable expansion tank is needed on the cold line.
  • Mixing valve. A thermostatic mixing valve listed to ASSE 1017 at the tank outlet lets you store water hotter and deliver it at a safe temperature. Water at 140°F can cause a serious scald in a few seconds.
  • Water quality. Stainless steel tanks have chloride limits stated in the warranty. Glass-lined steel tanks have an anode rod that needs periodic inspection.
  • Shutoff and drain valves on the cold inlet and at the base of the tank for service.

What to compare when buying

  • Tank material: stainless steel or glass-lined steel.
  • Coil surface area and pressure drop at rated flow.
  • First-hour and continuous ratings, and the boiler output they assume.
  • Connection locations: top or side, which affects how the tank fits next to the boiler.
  • Whether an aquastat or sensor well is included.
  • Height and diameter, including clearance for removing an anode or sensor.

When to call a professional

Adding an indirect means cutting into boiler piping, wiring boiler controls and working on a pressurized potable tank. A wrongly piped relief valve or a missing one is a serious hazard, and a miswired priority control can leave a house without heat. This is work for a licensed plumber or heating contractor, and a permit is usually required. Anything involving the boiler's gas supply, burner or venting must be done by a licensed professional. Owners can usefully prepare the sizing: count fixtures, note the boiler's rated output from its data plate and measure the space.
